RT-950 Pro audio limits: what data the radio can carry

The RT-950 Pro's two Beken BK4829 transceivers shape every bit of audio that goes in and out: filters, emphasis, a limiter. That's right for voice and wrong for data. We measured both directions on the bench, found the registers that switch the shaping off, and corrected what the hardware leaves behind. Measured on one radio (black, its UHF chip, 446.475 MHz) with a bladeRF as the far end.

Transmit

The radio's microcontroller feeds generated audio (APRS, test signals, future data modes) into the BK4829's audio input through a small resistor-capacitor network. Measured with the radio's own test signals (txtest): a third-octave tone sweep, the level of each tone against 1 kHz.

  • As set today (the data set-up that APRS bursts use): flat within 1 dB from 400 Hz to 2.5 kHz, then a wall: 3150 Hz −7 dB, 4 kHz −56 dB. Raising the TX low-pass (REG_43<8:6>) or switching off every REG_2B filter bit doesn't move it.
  • The filter bypass (REG_47 bit 0, "bypass all AF TX filters") removes the wall. What's left is the injection network: a first-order roll-off near 170 Hz, about 6 dB an octave down across the band, which also delays the low tones relative to the high ones (a near-constant −90° of phase).
  • The equaliser is a filter in our own DSP, between the generators and the DAC, designed from the measured channel: it boosts and re-times so that level and phase come out flat. eq covers 100 Hz–5 kHz at full deviation; eq8 reaches 8 kHz for data signals (a full-level 8 kHz sine would clip the DAC, so its sweep runs at level 29). The level lost to the bypass is won back through the chip's mic sensitivity (REG_7D) and deviation (REG_40).
  • Level alone wasn't enough. A first equaliser that fixed only the level made tones flat but left 9600 at 23 % bit errors: the network's phase still smeared each bit into the next. Measuring the channel's full response from captures of known bit patterns, and correcting phase too, brought it to 0.3 %.

Receive

The bladeRF sends a flat-modulated FM tone sweep; the radio's audio comes to the PC as a raw, gain-free 12-bit capture of exactly what its decoders see (16 kHz for sweeps).

  • As set today: a steep 300 Hz high-pass (−50 dB at 100 Hz) and de-emphasis, about 6 dB an octave down above 1 kHz (−8.5 dB at 2.5 kHz). Right for voice.
  • The three REG_2B receive filters off (300 Hz high-pass, 3 kHz low-pass, de-emphasis): flat from 800 Hz to 2.5 kHz. Below that, the board's coupling between the chip and the microcontroller rolls off from about 600 Hz (de-emphasis had been hiding it); no register moves it. Above 3.1 kHz the chip keeps a fixed wall that the low-pass bit doesn't remove.
  • Route 9 (REG_47 bits 11:8 = 9, the routine Beken's BK4819 reference manual calls RF_EnterBypass, "for digital walkie-talkies") takes the audio from before that wall: 3150 Hz goes from −10 to −2.4 dB, 4 kHz from −33 to −5.4 dB. The rest is a smooth slope a decoder can correct. It's unfiltered on the speaker too, so it belongs on a muted data slot.
  • Channel width isn't the limit: narrow FM (12.5 kHz) measured worse both ways, and the walls stayed put.
  • 9600 receive: not possible today, and unproven. G3RUH 9600 needs the audio close to flat to about 4.8 kHz and down near DC. Even with route 9 the receive path is about −9 dB at 4.8 kHz and −24 dB at 6.3 kHz, and the board's coupling takes the low end from 600 Hz down. There is no 9600 decoder on the radio, the PC capture can't carry it (12–16 kHz is under two samples a bit), and the chip's own modem finds no sync above 2400. Whether a decoder with heavy equalisation could recover it is an open question, not a result.

A real decoder with clock recovery, error correction (POCSAG fixes two bits a codeword) and a correction for the board's coupling would do better than this slicer; receive-side equalisation, as on transmit, is the next step. 9600 receive can't be judged through the PC: 12–16 kHz is under two samples a bit.

What Ferrite does with it

  • In the next release, APRS receive (APRS-55): on the APRS slot the 300 Hz high-pass, de-emphasis, noise gate and AFC are off. On a corpus of recorded APRS traffic this decoded about 1 % more frames; the on-air check is pending.
  • A transmit bug, fixed: DTMF detection and the tone generator share REG_70, and the key-up never disarmed detection. With APRS mode off, every voice transmission carried 1000 Hz and 3300 Hz tones at 1.2–1.5 kHz deviation. Now detection is off before every key-up (TX-49); verified clean on air.
  • On the bench, behind test commands: the transmit bypass and equalisers, the receive route 9 and filter hold, the FSK modem test, and a raw receive capture. These become data-slot set-ups as decoders arrive, starting with POCSAG and GMSK 2400; 9600 would be transmit only unless receive is shown to work.
  • Unchanged: voice keeps the chip's voice filters, and APRS 1200 transmit is already flat where its tones sit (1200 and 2200 Hz within 0.2 dB).

How this was measured, and its limits

  • One radio (black, UHF chip), one frequency (446.475 MHz), wide FM. Transmit tests with Dry TX (the power amplifier at zero) except one low-power run; the bladeRF 1 on a nearby PC as receiver or transmitter (its lowest transmit gain). It tunes from 237.5 MHz up, so 2 m wasn't measured.
  • Receive audio is a raw capture of the decoders' samples (12 kHz, or 16 kHz for sweeps), with no gain stage.
  • Registers were changed live on the bench; the radio's normal settings are untouched unless stated.
  • Bit error rates use a deliberately plain demodulator, so they show the channel, not a decoder's cleverness.
  • Tools: ferrite/tools/bench-blade/. Every test and its verdict is in the log below.
